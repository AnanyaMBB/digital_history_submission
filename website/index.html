01 — The research question

Across Britain's crises from 1847 to 1996, did the Bank of England manage financial stress through an open public market — or through a narrow recognised access network?

The whole project turns on that one distinction. To answer it, the paper asks three smaller questions, each tied to a different period and a different test.

Q1

Was there a Victorian inner circle?

Did a stable set of intermediaries sit at the front of the crisis information chain in the Victorian period, closest to the Bank?

1847 – 1890
Q2

Did the telegraph break it?

Did the 1866 transatlantic cable, which made prices travel faster, shrink that advantage and open access to the Bank?

1857 vs 1907
Q3

Did the structure survive?

Did the access network outlast the First World War, from Victorian bill brokers to later authorised money-market counterparties?

1914 – 1997
02 — Why this matters

Not just a banking question. A question about power, information, and access.

If markets were becoming modern and efficient, we might expect access to the Bank to grow more open over time. But if London finance stayed a closed access network, then modernity did not simply mean democratization. It meant older elite relationships were formalized into institutional rules.

If it were an open market

Anyone could reach the Bank directly. Access widens and diffuses. Crisis support is a public facility, open on equal terms.

vs
If it were an access network

A recognised gatekeeper layer rings the Bank. The wider public sits outside it. Crisis support flows through known intermediaries.

03 — The scholarly debate

Two ways of reading financial crises.

The project enters a real disagreement. One tradition sees finance as shaped by elite networks and private access. Another sees information becoming faster and markets becoming efficient. The paper takes both seriously.

Side A

The closed access club

Finance was shaped by elite networks, private information, and privileged access.

Hopkins (1988)

"Gentlemanly capitalism": a London elite whose advantage was relational, built on geographical location and social ties.

Amini & Toms (2021)

Interlocking directorates worked as private information conduits, giving some directors a better informational advantage.

Gorton & Ordoñez (2014)

The theory that a crisis is fundamentally an information event, where who knows what, and when, matters.

Flandreau & Ugolini (2011)

Victorian crisis liquidity ran through the discount-house and bill-broker layer, with privileged access to the Bank.

Side B

The efficient, faster market

Information became faster, prices became efficient, and simple insider-profit stories can be myths.

Hoag (2006) · Richmond Fed (2018)

The 1866 cable cut the London to New York information lag "from ten days to zero" and compressed price gaps from 5–10% to 2–3%.

Schneider (2022)

The Bank's 1866 lending was politically negotiated and deliberately ambiguous, not a transparent public rule.

Rothschild Archive (2014)

The famous insider-profit legend is "more legend than fact", a caution against assuming early access became profit.

The lesson

Measure dated visibility and access. Never assume foreknowledge or profit.

The turn

Maybe both are true. Prices became faster — but access to the Bank remained mediated.

Price information and institutional access are two different things. The cable settled the first. This project tests the second.

04 — The actors

The project follows actor types, not abstract data.

A crisis is easier to understand as a small cast of institutions than as a wall of numbers. The Bank of England sits at the centre. Around it are the firms that could reach the Bank, the banks that funded them, the press that made stress public, and the wider public that usually learned last.

The spine · 1830s – 1997

Discount houses

They buy and hold short bills, funded by overnight money lent by the banks. The same role runs down the middle of the whole story: the bill broker the Bank gave a facility to in 1830 becomes the authorised counterparty of 1996.

Revealed by · BoE transaction ledgers + the 1967 discount-market history
The original intermediaries · early 19th c.

Bill brokers

They matched lenders and borrowers of short IOUs, called bills of exchange. They are the ancestors of the discount houses and the first firms the Bank dealt with on a recognised footing.

Revealed by · ledgers + the 1906 bill-market reconstruction
The screeners · 19th c. – 1930s

Acceptance houses

Also called merchant banks. They guarantee, or "accept", a bill for a distant borrower, lending their name rather than their cash. They vet borrowers and form part of the recognised layer.

Revealed by · the 1914 ledger + the 1906 bill reconstruction
The recognised core · 1900s onward

Clearing banks

Large ordinary deposit banks that supply call money to the discount houses and later partner in rescues. They fund the network and help organise support.

Revealed by · Römer's 1931 study + the 1978 lifeboat account
The insider clock · Victorian – interwar

Specialist City press

The Bankers' Magazine and similar titles reported money-market strain for professionals. They show stress was legible to insiders before it reached the public.

Revealed by · The Bankers' Magazine
The public clock · every period

Newspapers & the public

HMD newspapers and Hansard form the broad public record. This is the comparison point the whole argument turns on: the moment a crisis became visible to everyone.

07 — Method · why crises, and why these

Why study a system through its worst days?

It is a fair objection that crises are unusual. The reason to study them is that a crisis makes a hidden structure visible. In calm years the access network is quiet. A discount house rolls over its bills, a clearing bank lends spare cash overnight, a trusted firm gets credit because everyone knows its name. These relationships matter, but they rarely leave a dated public trace. A panic makes them loud.

In calm years

The wiring stays hidden

Access runs on trust and habit. It is real, and it shapes who gets credit, but it produces little that you can date or count from the outside.

a crisis lights it up
In a panic

The wiring lights up

The Bank's ledgers, its rate moves, its rescue operations, the newspapers and the official record all turn dense and datable at the same moment. You can finally see who reaches the Bank, who moves early, who is supported, and who is left outside the recognised layer.

The claim is not that the network existed only during panics. It is that a panic forces private relationships into the record. So a crisis works as a stress test that exposes the wiring, and comparing several of them lets us ask whether the same structure keeps reappearing.

So why these crises, and not others?

The cases were not chosen for drama or severity, and the paper does not claim they are the most severe. They were chosen to vary along the dimensions that matter for the question: period, crisis origin (home-grown or imported), the communication technology of the day, and above all what kind of evidence survives about who could reach the Bank. Each case has to clear three bars at once.

Criterion 01

Historically consequential

Each is named in the scholarly literature as a defining moment for the Bank's crisis role, so the comparison engages real cases other historians argue about, not convenient ones.

Criterion 02

Structurally distinct

The cases sit at different points on the map: home-grown bill-market collapse, imported transatlantic shock, single-firm rescue, war and payment rupture. If one Bank rule ran uniformly, their shapes would converge. They do not.

Criterion 03

Comparable evidence

Each appears in the daily Bank Rate and the public record, and most carry a deeper source — a transaction ledger, daily account books, or a recent archival reconstruction — so they can be set side by side.

And deliberately left out
  • 1825 — predates the 1844 Bank Charter Act regime and the weekly balance-sheet series the project relies on; no comparable transaction source.
  • 1878 · City of Glasgow Bank — recorded as a crisis the Bank quietly prevented; a prevented non-panic leaves almost no acute-window footprint to measure.
  • 1899–1902 · Boer War — war finance, a different analytical category from a lender-of-last-resort panic, and a faint crisis footprint in the balance sheet.
  • 1912–13 · Balkan Wars — part of the run-up to 1914 rather than a separate episode; the 1913 observations serve as the 1914 baseline.

Choosing structurally distinct cases on purpose commits the argument to a particular shape. It cannot be a tidy ranking of more or less modern rescues. It has to be that the form of the crisis shapes who can reach the Bank — and where a single uniform rule had operated, the measures across these cases would converge rather than diverge. With a dozen cases the paper compares structures and traces continuity; it does not claim a statistical law over all crises or all central banks.     13 — The telegraph test

    Did the 1866 cable open the club?

    If the City's advantage were only about faster news, the transatlantic cable should have weakened it. Before 1866, market information crossed the Atlantic slowly, by ship. After the cable, a price could travel in minutes.

    14 — The recurring inner circle

    The front of the chain was not random.

    The exact firms changed. The actor type persisted.

    Of the ten intermediaries with the highest access scores, all ten reached the Bank's window early. They were mainly discount houses, bill brokers, and merchant banks, exactly the kinds of firms named in the City's own histories. Overend, Gurney recurs until it fails in 1866. Frühling & Göschen and Stern Bros reappear in 1914.

    This is not a claim that the same firms controlled everything. Membership turns over. The durable feature is the recognised layer, not any single name.

    15 — The benefit test

    Early access is not the same thing as profit.

    If listed financial firms were being repriced before a public rupture, the Yale Investor's Monthly Manual index should fall. It does not. In 1873, 1890, and 1907 the sector was still rising into the rupture.

    The project finds an access advantage, not a proven profit advantage.

    1

    Bank of England lender-of-last-resort transaction ledgers

    Anson, Bholat, Kang & Thomas (2017) · BoE Staff Working Paper 691
    ▾

    Where it came from

    The Bank of England, transcribed from its own archival discount ledgers (the C28 series) by a named team and published alongside the 2017 working paper.

    What it contains

    Loan-by-loan rows for the 1847, 1857, 1866 and 1914 crises: discounter name, amount, rate, collateral, and whether the bill was accepted or rejected.

    Why the project needed it

    It is the only source that names who actually reached the Bank's discount window during a crisis, which is the heart of the access question.

    How it was processed

    Reshaped into a long-form table, then names were resolved from 1,476 raw spellings to 1,314 entities and classified by type (discount house, bill broker, merchant bank, clearing bank).

    What it showed

    The concentration and breadth of crisis lending: 1866 had the broadest distribution (top-five share 19%, HHI 154), while 1857 was far more concentrated (top-five 42%, HHI 503). It also names the recurring inner circle.

    Limitations

    No 1890 ledger exists in the release. The 1914 ledger has fewer columns (11 versus 28). 86% of resolved names still carry a manual-review flag, so type shares are lower-bound estimates. The ledger records the what of lending, never the why.

    Daily Bank Rate, from A Millennium of Macroeconomic Data

    Bank of England research dataset · sheet D1
    ▾

    Where it came from

    The Bank of England's long-run macro compilation, which records the Bank's announced policy rate at daily frequency back to 1833.

    What it contains

    One row per rate announcement: date and the end-of-day Bank Rate in percent per year.

    Why the project needed it

    It provides a single, uniform daily clock that every crisis can be measured against, and the rate level needed for the penalty-rate test.

    How it was processed

    The raw sheet holds 67,578 rows because of intra-day announcements; these were de-duplicated by date to give one settled daily rate.

    What it showed

    The earliest market-facing signal date for each crisis, and confirmation that the Bank charged a penalty rate in three of the four core crises.

    Limitations

    It is the Bank's own policy rate, not the market discount rate faced by counterparties, so it cannot speak to the spread individual firms actually paid.

    Three digitised text corpora: mass-market newspapers, the parliamentary record, and the specialist financial press for professionals.

    What it contains

    Dated, searchable text in which a crisis is named or discussed, at three levels of audience from insider to public.

    Yale Investor's Monthly Manual securities panel

    Yale School of Management, cleaned digitised files
    ▾

    Where it came from

    A cleaned, digitised version of the Investor's Monthly Manual, a nineteenth- and twentieth-century publication reporting listed securities.

    What it contains

    One row per listed security per month: company name, industry code, security type, and the late-month quoted price where available.

    Why the project needed it

    It gives an independent market view of ordinary financial firms, separate from the Bank's own records, to test whether prices moved before a rupture.

    How it was processed

    Filtered to common and preferred stocks, split into listed financial institutions and non-financial corporates, with monthly returns and the share of declining securities computed.

    What it showed

    No clear pre-rupture price footprint. In November 1890 listed financial institutions returned about −0.9% against −1.2% for non-financial corporates, a mild and not distinctive move.

    Limitations

    Monthly and sector-level; quotes are often stale; several key discount houses were never listed; and the panel has a severe quotation gap around 1914.

    The 1906 sterling bills reconstruction

    Accominotti, Lucena-Piquero & Ugolini
    ▾

    Where it came from

    A large scholarly reconstruction of the prewar London bill market built from rediscounted sterling bills.

    What it contains

    Counts and relationships across tens of thousands of bills, linking the firms that drew, accepted, and rediscounted them.

    Why the project needed it

    It maps the prewar access funnel: how a very wide base of drawers narrowed to a small set of names that reached the Bank.

    How it was processed

    Used as a published structure to characterise the funnel from many drawers down to a recognised discounting layer.

    What it showed

    A steep narrowing: a broad population of drawers passed through acceptance houses to a small recognised group of discounting firms at the apex.

    Limitations

    1906 is a normal year, not a crisis year, so it describes the standing structure rather than crisis behaviour directly.

    BoE transaction ledgers

    Rung 3 · loan-level
    A digitised transcription of the Bank's own archival discount ledgers.

    Obtained from

    Published by the Bank of England with Anson, Bholat, Kang & Thomas (2017).

    Created by

    Bank clerks who recorded each discount; transcribed by a named team from the archival C28 series.

    Curated / digitised

    Hand-transcribed from page images into a spreadsheet, with raw transactions and pre-computed metrics.

    Why reliable

    Working records of the Bank itself, with an independent secondary cross-check on the 1866 portion.

    Biases / weaknesses

    Clerks' name spellings vary; no 1890 ledger; the 1914 ledger is thinner; classification still mostly flagged for review.

    Cannot claim

    It cannot show motive or decision rationale, a counterparty's wider balance sheet, or any credit that did not pass through the Bank's window.

    Daily Bank Rate

    Rung 2 · daily policy rate
    The Bank's own announced rate, digitised to daily frequency.

    Obtained from

    A Millennium of Macroeconomic Data, the Bank's long-run compilation.

    Created by

    The Bank of England, drawing the rate from its internal and published records.

    Curated / digitised

    Daily observations digitised into a single sheet, then de-duplicated by date for this project.

    Why reliable

    The authoritative record of the Bank's own rate, used to cross-check the 1890 image transcription.

    Biases / weaknesses

    It is a policy rate, not a market rate, and intra-day changes had to be settled to one daily value.

    Cannot claim

    It cannot describe the open-market discount rates that counterparties actually faced.

    Rung 1 · dated text
    Three digitised corpora at three levels of audience.

    Obtained from

    British Library digitised newspapers, the Hansard parliamentary record, and Internet Archive runs of the specialist press.

    Created by

    Contemporary editors, reporters, and parliamentary recorders.

    Curated / digitised

    Scanned and OCR'd by their respective libraries and archives.

    Why reliable

    Contemporary printed records, dated at the point of publication.

    Biases / weaknesses

    Survival, scanning selection, and OCR quality all shape which pages are present and findable.

    Obtained from

    Cleaned files published by the Yale School of Management.

    Curated / digitised

    Filtered to common and preferred stocks and split into financial and non-financial sectors.

    Institutional record · 1967 & 1978

    Obtained from

    The Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin, 1967 and 1978 Q2.

    Created by

    The Bank of England's own staff.

    Curated / digitised

    Published institutional accounts, read here as primary sources on structure and membership.

    Why reliable

    Written by the institution that ran the market and organised the rescue, with named participants and amounts.

    Institutional authorship: the Bank narrating its own market and its own rescue.

    BoE Daily Account Books C1/38

    Primary image · 1890
    Photographs of the Bank's daily balance-sheet ledger, transcribed by a vision model.

    Obtained from

    The Bank of England Archive, via its digital image service.

    Created by

    Bank clerks recording the daily Banking and Issue Department balances in 1890.

    Curated / digitised

    Eighteen page images for October to December 1890, transcribed by a vision model after OCR proved too weak.

    Why reliable

    The Bank's own daily record; the transcription validates against weekly anchors at 92 to 100% for the variables kept.

    Biases / weaknesses

    Only three months are transcribed, and only a few balance-sheet variables passed validation.

    Cannot claim

    It carries no counterparty identities and supports no cross-crisis daily comparison.

    Source absence is not historical absence. A gap in the data this project uses, such as the missing 1890 transaction ledger, is a limit of the project's coverage, not proof that the underlying record never existed. Where a claim cannot be traced through this chain, the project does not make it.

    Methods · how the analysis works

    Ten methods, in plain terms.

    No finance background needed. Each method below names the question it answers, the data it uses, how it works in simple language, what it produces, and where it can mislead.

    Method 01

    Transaction-ledger analysis

    Who actually borrowed from the Bank during a crisis?

    Data
    BoE transaction ledgers (1847 / 57 / 66 / 1914).
    How
    Read every loan as a row, then total and rank borrowing by firm within each crisis window.
    Output
    A ranked list of the firms closest to the Bank's window in each crisis.
    Limit
    Only four crises have ledgers, and 1914's is thinner.
    Method 02

    Counterparty classification

    What kind of institution was each borrower?

    Data
    The cleaned ledger names.
    How
    Match messy spellings to one canonical name, then label each as a discount house, bill broker, merchant bank, clearing bank, or other.
    Output
    A type for each firm, so borrowing can be grouped by actor type.
    Limit
    Most labels are flagged for review, so type shares are lower bounds.
    Method 03

    Recurrence analysis

    Do the same firms or firm types keep reappearing?

    Data
    The classified ledgers across crises.
    How
    Count how many crises each firm and each firm type appears in near the front of the chain.
    Output
    A recurrence score showing which actors persist over time.
    Limit
    Firm names turn over, so the durable unit is the type, not the name.
    Method 04

    Lead-time analysis

    How early was stress visible to the market versus the public?

    Data
    Daily Bank Rate plus dated newspaper and Hansard mentions.
    How
    For each crisis, find the earliest market-facing signal date and the public rupture date, then measure the gap in days.
    Output
    A lead time per crisis, from 1 day to 288 days.
    Limit
    It measures dated visibility, not what anyone knew or did.
    Method 05

    Text mining for visibility clocks

    When did each crisis become speakable, and to whom?

    Data
    Digitised newspapers, Hansard, and the specialist press.
    How
    Search dated text for when each crisis is first named, at insider and public levels.
    Output
    Two visibility clocks, specialist and public, to compare against the market signal.
    Limit
    Scanning and OCR gaps can move a first-mention date.
    Method 06

    Concentration and funnel analysis

    How narrow was access to the Bank?

    Data
    Ledger totals by firm, plus the 1906 bills reconstruction.
    How
    Measure how much borrowing the top few firms account for, using top-five share and a concentration index.
    Output
    Concentration figures per crisis and a picture of the prewar funnel.
    Limit
    Concentration describes structure, not intent.
    Method 07

    Source-derived actor-flow networks

    How did liquidity move between institutions?

    Data
    Ledgers for 1857 / 66 / 1914; published studies for 1931 and 1973–75.
    How
    Draw actors as nodes and flows as links, from raw rows where available and from the literature where not.
    Output
    Network diagrams showing the Bank, its intermediaries, and the wider market.
    Limit
    The 1931 and 1973–75 networks are derived from studies, not raw data.
    Method 08

    Time-series summary

    How did the Bank's balance sheet move through a crisis?

    Data
    Weekly balance sheet and daily Bank Rate.
    How
    Line up each crisis on a shared clock and compare lending, reserves, and rate around the trigger.
    Output
    Event-time charts that make crises comparable side by side.
    Limit
    Weekly data cannot resolve within-week timing.
    Method 09

    Continuity-index scoring

    Was the access structure open or mediated, and how sure can we be?

    Data
    All of the above, per period.
    How
    Score six yes-or-no criteria for each period: four about structure, two about how much we can trust the reading.
    Output
    A comparable score per period, high from 1847 to 1996, then dropping at 1997.
    Limit
    The criteria are a deliberate simplification of a richer record.
    Method 10

    Price-proxy test

    Did markets reprice financial firms before a public rupture?

    Data
    Yale Investor's Monthly Manual prices.
    How
    Track listed financial-sector prices into each rupture and compare against non-financial firms.
    Output
    A test of whether an access advantage left a visible price footprint.
    Limit
    Monthly, sector-level, and blind to unlisted firms, so a null result is not proof of no benefit.

    What it shows

    Comparing two US-origin shocks, the insider-to-public lead grew from 25 days in 1857 to 68 days in 1907, after the 1866 transatlantic cable made prices travel in minutes.

    If the advantage were only about fast news, the lead should have shrunk. It widened, so price speed and access ran on different channels.

    The research question

    An open market, or an access network?

    Across Britain's crises from 1847 to 1996, did the Bank of England manage stress through an open public market, or through a narrow recognised access network?
    If markets became modern and efficient, access should have opened. The evidence suggests a different story: prices became faster, while access stayed mediated.
    Q1 Did a Victorian inner circle sit at the front of the chain?
    Q2 Did the 1866 telegraph weaken that advantage?
    Q3 Did the structure survive into the twentieth century?
    The scholarly debate

    The project tests whether faster information also meant open access.
